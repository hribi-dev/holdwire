**Short version:** The World Bank Group said on Tuesday, 17 November, that it will explore paying out development funds in regulated US dollar stablecoins, working with Citi and Circle, the issuer of USDC. This is a study, not a launch: no timeline, pilot countries or amounts have been published. It still matters, because one of the world's largest development lenders is now looking at stablecoins as a way to move public money.

## What happened

The World Bank Group wants to test blockchain-based payment rails alongside correspondent banking, the chain of intermediary banks that carries its payments today, The Block reports. The stated aim is disbursements that are faster, cheaper and easier to trace, according to Decrypt.

Each partner has a defined role. Citi brings its connections to established banking rails. Circle provides the infrastructure to mint and settle funds in USDC, The Block reports.

The first phase will focus on what the World Bank calls an intermediated and interoperable payment model. In plain terms, regulated institutions stay in the middle, and the system is meant to work with other networks. Work will start with borrower countries where digital assets are permitted, and other banks may join later, according to Decrypt.

The project builds on FundsChain, the World Bank's existing blockchain platform for tracking how project money is spent. FundsChain covers more than 350 projects in 96 countries, The Block reports.

World Bank Group president Ajay Banga framed the effort as improving "the financial plumbing of development", Decrypt reports. His examples were practical: a clinic built on time, or a farmer paid before the harvest season ends.

## Why it matters

**The sums involved are large.** The World Bank Group delivered $123.5 billion in loans, grants, equity investments and guarantees in the fiscal year to 30 June 2026, according to The Block. Nobody has said how much money would move this way, or whether the test will lead to regular use. Even so, a modest share of flows on that scale would be significant for stablecoin payments.

**It shifts the stablecoin story towards public institutions.** Stablecoins are best known for crypto trading and private payments. A multilateral lender owned by 189 member countries testing them for disbursements is a different kind of use.

**It comes while stablecoin supply is shrinking.** The combined market value of USDT, USDC, BUSD and DAI was about $250 billion on Tuesday, down from a peak of $273 billion in May, according to Glassnode data cited by CoinDesk. USDC itself stands at about $68.9 billion, CoinMarketCap reports. The World Bank news doesn't change that near-term picture, because an exploration adds no demand by itself.

**Banks are building their own alternatives.** Citi is working with Circle here, but it is also among the banks behind a [$270 million bank-led stablecoin project](/blog/banks-270-million-stablecoin-project-faro-what-it-means-2026-11-11). Large institutions are testing several routes at once and have not settled on one.

## What this means for you

- **Nothing changes for your own payments.** This concerns money moving between the World Bank, governments and projects. It is not a consumer product.
- **There is no "World Bank coin".** The project uses USDC, an existing stablecoin. Treat any token or investment offer that uses the World Bank's name as a likely scam. Our lesson on [spotting scams](/school/spotting-scams) covers the warning signs.
- **USDC is designed to hold $1.** News like this is not a reason to expect a stablecoin to rise in value, because its purpose is to stay at its peg.
- **EU rules still apply here.** Stablecoins offered in the EU fall under MiCA, whatever a global institution does elsewhere. See [MiCA explained](/learn/mica-explained).
- **The dollar focus is worth noticing.** The test uses dollar stablecoins, which adds to the debate in Europe about dollar tokens dominating digital payments. Our [fintech section](/fintech) follows what reaches European consumers.

## What to watch

- Which borrower countries are named first, and how their regulators respond.
- Whether the World Bank publishes cost and speed results compared with correspondent banking.
- Which other banks join after Citi.
- Whether the World Bank later considers stablecoins in other currencies, including the euro.
