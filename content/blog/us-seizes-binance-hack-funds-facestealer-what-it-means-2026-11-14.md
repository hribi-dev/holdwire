**Short version:** The US Justice Department said on 12 November that a court has authorised it to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the crypto stolen from Binance on 29 October, the largest crypto theft on record. On the same day, the FBI said it had taken down the servers behind FaceStealer, a North Korean scam that used fake video calls and deepfakes to steal more than $400 million over about 18 months. The money is not back with anyone yet, and the scam method is still worth knowing, because it targets ordinary crypto users as well as founders.

## What happened

The Justice Department announced two linked actions against North Korea's crypto theft operations, according to Security Affairs and The Hacker News.

**The Binance money.** The FBI has blamed the 29 October Binance hack, estimated at $6.3 billion, on TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking unit tied to the country's military, Al Jazeera reported. Government agencies and private companies have now frozen more than $2.7 billion of it, about half of the total, according to the Justice Department's computer crime section as quoted by Security Affairs. A court has authorised the US to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of that frozen amount.

**FaceStealer.** This is the FBI's name for a social-engineering campaign it attributes to BlueNoroff, a North Korean group also tracked as APT38 and Sapphire Sleet. Agents seized the cloud servers used to run it and, under a federal warrant, more than 900 webcam recordings of victims, The Hacker News reports. The FBI has published a report and a page for possible victims at fbi.gov/facestealer.

## How the scam worked

According to the FBI's description, as summarised by The Hacker News, the scheme repeats in a loop:

1. The attackers take over someone's account, often on Telegram, and record their video calls.
2. From that trusted account, they invite the victim's contacts to a "Zoom" or "Teams" meeting that is in fact fake.
3. In the meeting, they play recorded clips or deepfakes of people the target knows, so the call looks real.
4. They then fake an audio problem and offer a "fix", a link or command that installs malware.
5. The malware steals wallets and seed phrases, and the new victim's accounts are used to hit the next round of contacts.

Some victims were compromised more than a year before their money was taken, Security Affairs reports. Security firm Arctic Wolf counted 677 targets across 58 countries in its latest update, more than 80% of them founders or senior executives. The FBI also warned that the group targets software developers and popular code libraries.

## Why it matters

Frozen is not the same as returned. Bitdefender points out that frozen or restrained funds are not necessarily recovered funds: forfeiture takes court time, and the hackers are still moving the rest through bridges, mixers and exchanges, Security Affairs reports. Still, freezing about half of a $6.3 billion theft within two weeks is unusually fast. It shows how much of the industry, from stablecoin issuers to exchanges and analytics firms, now co-operates to block stolen coins.

FaceStealer matters for a different reason. It does not break any wallet or exchange. It breaks trust between people who know each other, which no software update can fully fix. US officials say the proceeds fund North Korea's weapons programme, according to The Hacker News, so the pressure to keep doing it is high.

## What this means for you

- **Treat "fix your audio" as a red flag.** No legitimate meeting needs you to paste a command into your computer or install a plugin from a chat link. Leave the call and reconnect through the app you normally use.
- **Verify on a second channel.** If a contact sends an unexpected meeting invite, especially about money, a deal or a job, check with them by phone or a different app first. A familiar face on screen is no longer proof.
- **Keep your seed phrase off any computer.** Malware like this looks for wallet files and recovery phrases. A hardware wallet with the phrase written down offline limits the damage.
- **Exchange users were not asked to act.** Nothing in this week's announcement requires anything from Binance customers. If you are owed anything, rely only on Binance's official channels: seizure news is a favourite hook for refund scams.

## What to watch

- Formal forfeiture filings for the $1.2 billion, and whether any of it is returned to Binance.
- How much more of the remaining stolen funds gets frozen as investigators trace the laundering.
- Copycat versions of the fake-meeting trick now that the method is public.
