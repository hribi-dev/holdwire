## Wallets hold keys, not coins

Your coins always live on the blockchain. A **wallet** holds the **private key**: a secret that proves you're allowed to move them. Think of the blockchain as a street of glass mailboxes. Anyone can see what's inside, but only the key opens yours.

Your **address** is like your mailbox number. You can share it so people can send you crypto. Your private key you never share.

## Custodial vs self-custody

**Custodial.** An exchange or app holds the keys for you. You log in with a password, and if you forget it, support can help. The catch: you're trusting the company. If it's hacked, freezes withdrawals or goes bust, your crypto can be stuck or lost. FTX's collapse in 2022 is the famous example.

**Self-custody.** You hold the keys yourself in a wallet app or device. Nobody can freeze your funds. The catch: nobody can help you either. Lose the keys and the coins are gone.

You'll often hear "not your keys, not your coins". It's a fair warning, but self-custody brings its own risk. Many beginners start on a well-regulated exchange and learn self-custody once they're comfortable.

## Hot and cold wallets

- **Hot wallet:** an app on your phone or browser, connected to the internet. Handy for small amounts.
- **Cold wallet:** keys kept offline, usually on a **hardware wallet** (a small device). Much harder to hack. Better for larger amounts.

## The seed phrase

When you set up a self-custody wallet, it shows you **12 or 24 words**. This is the **seed phrase** (or recovery phrase). It can rebuild your wallet on any device, which makes it the master key to everything in it.

The rules:

1. **Write it down on paper** (or stamp it into metal). No screenshots, no cloud notes, no email.
2. **Store it somewhere safe** where it won't be lost, burned or found by others.
3. **Never type it into a website** or share it with anyone, including "support". No genuine company will ever ask for it.
4. **Check the address** before every transfer. Copy-paste malware can swap addresses.

Remember from the scams lesson: anyone asking for your seed phrase is trying to rob you.
