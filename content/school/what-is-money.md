## Money is an agreement

A €20 note is just printed paper. It has value because everyone around you agrees to accept it. That shared agreement lets money do three jobs:

- **Pay for things.** Instead of swapping eggs for a haircut, you pay with money.
- **Measure prices.** You can compare a phone and a bike because both have a price in euros.
- **Store value.** You can earn money today and spend it next month.

Today most money isn't even paper. It's a number in your bank's computer, and banks create most of it when they lend.

## Why prices keep rising

**Inflation** is the general rise in prices over time. When inflation is 2%, a basket of shopping that cost €100 last year costs about €102 now.

Prices rise for many reasons: more money chasing the same goods, higher energy costs, higher wages, or supply problems. Central banks try to keep inflation low and steady. In the euro area that's the job of the **European Central Bank (ECB)**, which aims for 2% a year over the medium term. When inflation runs too hot, the ECB usually raises interest rates to cool it down.

## Why this matters to you

Inflation is a quiet tax on money that does nothing. If your savings earn 1% while prices rise 3%, you're losing about 2% of buying power a year, even though the number in your account goes up.

That's the main reason people invest: to try to grow their money faster than prices rise. But investing always comes with risk, which is what the next lessons cover.

You'll also hear crypto fans say Bitcoin protects against inflation because its supply is capped. That's a claim worth testing, not a fact: Bitcoin's price has swung far more than inflation ever does. We'll look at it properly in Level 2.
