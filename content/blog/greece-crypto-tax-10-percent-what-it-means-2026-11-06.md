**Short version:** Greece's parliament on Thursday approved a 10% tax on gains from crypto, with the first €500 of net gains each year exempt, according to Reuters. It is the country's first full legal framework for taxing crypto. Some details, including audit and transparency rules, will only come in follow-up rules in the coming weeks.

## What happened

The bill passed on 5 November, Reuters reports. Until now Greece had no dedicated system for taxing crypto, which left investors and tax officials guessing.

The rate came down during the process. Officials told Reuters in June it would be 15%. The draft put out for public consultation in October proposed 10%, according to Decrypt, and that is the rate parliament approved.

What we know about how it works, from Crypto Briefing's summary of the law:

- **10% on net gains** for individuals, after losses. The first **€500 a year** is tax-free.
- **Selling for euros is taxable.** So is paying for goods or services with crypto, and putting crypto into a company's capital.
- **Swapping one coin for another is not taxed**, for example bitcoin into another token. Tax applies when you leave crypto, not when you move between coins.
- **Staking, lending and liquidity income** is treated as interest. Crypto Briefing reports a 10% rate for it; KuCoin's market report gives 15%. Check the final text or a tax adviser before relying on either figure.
- **Losses** above €500 can be carried forward for up to five years.
- **A 12-month window** to declare past gains that were never reported, without penalties if the tax is paid on time.

KuCoin's report says the rules apply from 1 January 2027. A senior finance ministry official told Reuters that further implementation rules, including on transparency and audits, will follow in the coming weeks.

## Why it matters

The EU has no single crypto tax. [MiCA](/learn/mica-explained) regulates the companies that serve you, but tax stays with each country. Rates range from about 8% to 30%, according to Reuters. Decrypt lists some examples: Cyprus charges a flat 8%, Spain taxes gains at up to 28%, Ireland and Italy at 33%, and Germany exempts coins held for more than a year. Greece's 10% puts it at the low end.

There is also a reporting angle. Since 1 January, EU rules known as DAC8 require crypto platforms to collect data on their EU customers' transactions and share it with each customer's tax authority, Decrypt notes. Greek officials told Reuters they could not estimate how much the tax will raise because most Greek investors use platforms based abroad. DAC8 is the tool that is meant to close that gap.

The contrast with France this week is striking. There, lawmakers' rejection of the budget's revenue section has shelved, for now, a plan to tax some crypto-to-stablecoin swaps and to make people report large self-custodied holdings ([our brief](/news/france-shelves-stablecoin-tax-self-custody-reporting-2026-11-06)).

## What this means for you

- **If you are tax-resident in Greece,** keep a record of every purchase, sale and spend from now on: date, amount, price in euros. You will need cost prices to work out net gains, and the voluntary disclosure window is a chance to fix past years.
- **If you live elsewhere in the EU,** nothing changes for you. Your own country's rules apply. But assume your platform reports to your tax office: under DAC8, it almost certainly does.
- **Coin-to-coin swaps** are untaxed in Greece, but not everywhere. In several EU countries a swap is a taxable event. Don't assume one country's rule holds in another.
- **Staking income** is treated differently from trading gains in most countries. Keep it separate in your records.

This is a summary of reported rules, not tax advice. For your own situation, speak to a tax adviser in your country.

## What to watch

- The implementing rules promised "in the coming weeks", which should settle the staking rate and how audits will work.
- Whether Greece's approach to swaps and its low rate influence debates elsewhere, including France's budget as it moves to the Senate.
- How the first DAC8 reports, covering this year, are used by tax authorities in 2027.
