**Short version:** Hong Kong's Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) added Hyperliquid to its list of suspicious virtual asset trading platforms on 18 November. The regulator says the platform is not licensed and is suspected of serving or marketing to people in Hong Kong. It is a public warning, not a ban, and HYPE barely moved.

## What happened

The SFC's alert names "Hyperliquid / Hyperliquid Hong Kong" and lists the trading site app.hyperliquid.xyz/trade, according to crypto.news. The regulator said Hyperliquid has no licence and is suspected of operating in Hong Kong or actively marketing its services to the Hong Kong public. 36Crypto adds that the SFC said Hyperliquid has no business address that can be publicly verified.

The listing does not block the website, remove an app, freeze assets or halt trading, crypto.news reports. It also does not accuse Hyperliquid of fraud. crypto.news said it asked Hyperliquid for comment and had no reply by press time.

Hong Kong is not the first. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority put Hyperliquid on its warning list on 21 May, and Singapore's Monetary Authority added it to its investor alert list on 26 June, according to crypto.news.

HYPE dipped to about $65.50 after the news and recovered to $68.79 by midday on 19 November, 36Crypto reports, citing TradingView data. crypto.news put it at $68.48, down 0.33% in 24 hours and down 12.62% over the month.

## Why it matters

Hyperliquid runs its own blockchain and order book, with no company that signs up customers in each country. That model has carried it far: it overtook Bybit, Kraken and OKX in open interest, The Block reported on 13 November. But regulators judge platforms by who they serve, not how they are built. A licence-based system like Hong Kong's has no easy place for a venue with no registered office.

The timing is pointed. Hong Kong is building out its own licensed crypto market. Financial Secretary Paul Chan said on 16 November that the city will pass laws before year-end covering crypto dealers, custodians, advisers and asset managers, 36Crypto reports. Licensed Hong Kong exchanges compete with offshore venues for the same traders.

At the same time, Hyperliquid is trying to engage. On 18 November its Hyperliquid Policy Center sent the UK FCA seven recommendations for Britain's coming crypto rules, 36Crypto reports. That suggests the project wants a seat at the table where rules are written, rather than only staying offshore.

## What this means for you

- **This is about Hong Kong, not the EU.** The SFC warning has no legal effect in Europe, and nothing about your funds on Hyperliquid changed on Wednesday.
- **But the same question applies here.** Under MiCA, firms that offer crypto services to people in the EU need to be authorised. Before you use any platform, check your national regulator's warnings and ESMA's register. Our [MiCA explainer](/learn/mica-explained) and the [exchanges and MiCA lesson](/school/exchanges-and-mica) show how.
- **Know what you give up on an unlicensed venue.** There is no compensation scheme, no complaints body and no local company to take to court if something goes wrong.
- **Self-custody does not remove regulatory risk.** A regulator can push app stores, payment providers or front-end hosts to cut access, even if the chain keeps running.

## What to watch

- Whether Hong Kong follows the warning with harder steps, such as asking for the site to be blocked.
- Any public response from Hyperliquid or its Policy Center.
- Whether other Asian regulators add Hyperliquid to their lists, and whether trading volume from the region drops.
- What the FCA does with Hyperliquid's seven recommendations. Follow it on our [Hyperliquid hub](/hyperliquid).
